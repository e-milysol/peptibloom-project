## Editorial purpose

A practical Research Basics candidate explaining why “alcohol” is not a single disinfection condition. The intended public value is to help readers distinguish concentration, contact time, surface/skin context, formulation and organism susceptibility without turning a general educational article into a protocol or medical recommendation.

This draft is deliberately conservative. It does not prescribe a peptide-handling workflow, sterile-compounding method, injection preparation, dose, treatment or clinical practice.

## Draft article

### “Alcohol” is not one disinfection condition

Alcohol-based disinfectants are common because ethanol and isopropyl alcohol can inactivate many microorganisms quickly under suitable conditions. But a bottle label saying “alcohol” does not by itself tell you whether a disinfection step is appropriate or effective.

The result depends on several variables at once: which alcohol is present, its concentration, how long the target remains adequately wet, whether organic material is present, what surface or tissue is being treated, and which microorganisms are relevant.

That is why concentration should be treated as one part of a validated disinfection condition rather than as a stand-alone guarantee.

### More concentrated is not automatically better

For healthcare environmental disinfection, CDC guidance describes ethyl and isopropyl alcohol as rapidly bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic against vegetative bacteria and notes activity against several other microorganism classes, while also stating that they do not destroy bacterial spores. The CDC discussion describes the optimum bactericidal concentration as a 60%–90% solution in water by volume.

Water is therefore not merely an unwanted dilution in this context. Effective alcohol disinfection depends on the formulation and use condition, and very high concentration should not be assumed to outperform an established validated range.

This does **not** mean that every product between 60% and 90% is interchangeable. Product formulation, intended use, required contact time and applicable regulatory instructions still matter.

### Contact time matters

A disinfectant must remain in effective contact with its target for the required time. Alcohols evaporate quickly, which is useful in some settings but can also make adequate wet contact harder to maintain on larger surfaces.

A practical consequence is that a concentration value without the associated product instructions and contact conditions is incomplete information. “I used alcohol” is not a reproducible disinfection record.

### Cleaning and disinfection are different operations

Visible contamination and organic material can interfere with disinfection processes. A disinfection claim should not silently substitute for whatever cleaning step is required by the validated procedure or product instructions.

For public educational content, it is safer to preserve the distinction: cleaning removes material; disinfection is a separate microbial-control step performed under defined conditions.

### Alcohol has important limits

Alcohol is not a universal sterilant. CDC guidance explicitly notes that ethyl and isopropyl alcohol do not destroy bacterial spores and discusses limitations that make alcohol unsuitable for sterilizing medical and surgical materials.

Susceptibility also differs among microorganism classes and formulations. Readers should therefore avoid translating a broad statement such as “alcohol kills germs” into a claim that a particular organism, material or procedure has been validated.

### Skin antisepsis is not the same question as surface disinfection

Products and procedures intended for hand hygiene or skin antisepsis are governed by their own formulations, indications and instructions. Likewise, environmental surface disinfectants have their own use conditions. A concentration cited in one context should not be copied into another as if context were irrelevant.

For any real procedure, the controlling source should be the approved product label, applicable professional/institutional procedure, and relevant regulatory or infection-control guidance—not an isolated concentration remembered from a general article.

### What to record when evaluating a disinfection claim

A useful evidence-oriented checklist is:

- active alcohol(s) and concentration;
- formulation/product identity;
- intended use: hands/skin, environmental surface, equipment, or another defined context;
- required wet/contact time;
- cleaning/preparation requirements;
- target organisms or validated efficacy claim;
- material compatibility and safety constraints;
- authoritative instructions or evidence supporting the claim.

If those details are missing, uncertainty should be stated rather than filled by assumption.

## What this article does not establish

- It does not establish a sterile technique for peptide preparation or administration.
- It does not validate any specific commercial disinfectant.
- It does not establish that a given concentration is effective against every pathogen.
- It does not replace a product label, healthcare procedure, infection-control policy or regulatory instruction.
- It does not authorize publication of a scientific claim before Evidence and Scientific QA review.

## Source candidates for Evidence qualification

1. U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), *Guideline for Disinfection and Sterilization in Healthcare Facilities* — Chemical Disinfectants / Alcohol section. Candidate authority for alcohol spectrum, 60%–90% bactericidal range, evaporation/material limitations and non-sporicidal limitation.
2. World Health Organization (WHO), *WHO Guidelines on Hand Hygiene in Health Care*. Candidate authority for alcohol-based handrub formulation/use context and the need not to collapse hand hygiene into environmental-surface disinfection.

These references are source candidates, not an Evidence acceptance decision. Evidence remains authoritative for scientific support, provenance, uncertainty and downstream eligibility.
